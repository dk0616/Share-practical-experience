I was the network engineer for a 118-person architectural consultancy in Manchester. Our office occupied two floors and included open-plan design areas, six meeting rooms, several hot-desk zones, a model workshop, and a client presentation suite.

On paper, the network looked healthy.

We had a 1 Gbps internet connection. Signal reached almost every desk. Employees could run respectable speed tests early in the morning.

At 10:30 on Tuesday, however, the complaints began.

Teams calls froze in two meeting rooms. Architects opening cloud-based project files waited several seconds longer than usual. Designers returning from meetings occasionally lost audio while walking back to their desks. Visitors in the presentation suite reported inconsistent guest WiFi.

Nothing had completely failed.

That made the problem harder to justify financially.

Our existing WiFi 5 network had been installed when the office employed around sixty people. Since then, the workforce had nearly doubled. More importantly, the number of devices per employee had changed.

Most staff now carried a laptop and phone. Some used tablets. Meeting rooms contained wireless displays and video-conferencing equipment. Printers, cameras, visitor devices, and several building systems also occupied the wireless environment.

The office had grown denser without becoming physically larger.

Management initially asked whether WiFi 6 would simply make everything faster.

That was the wrong question.

A UK system integrator surveyed the office during normal working hours and shared the results with COMFAST engineers. They focused on the busiest rooms, movement routes, client counts, switch capacity, cabling, PoE availability, and peak-hour behaviour.

The meeting rooms were especially revealing.

One fourteen-person conference room could contain nearly thirty wireless devices when a client session began. The old access point still showed strong signal, but devices were competing for airtime while video, screen sharing, messaging, and background synchronization occurred simultaneously.

The open office behaved differently. Users were spread across a larger area, so the weakness appeared mostly during traffic bursts.

This is where [enterprise WiFi 6 upgrade planning](https://wifioem.com/is-upgrading-to-enterprise-wifi-6-worth-it/ ) became useful. WiFi 6 was not treated as a faster replacement for old hardware. Its value was judged by whether better multi-user scheduling, capacity, and roaming would address problems employees were already experiencing.

We did not replace everything immediately.

The pilot covered the busiest meeting suite, one hot-desk area, and the design department. Six COMFAST WiFi 6 access points replaced the older units in those zones. Existing cable routes were checked, PoE capacity was verified, and the radio layout was revised rather than copying the old AP positions.

COMFAST engineers supported the integrator remotely with channel planning, transmit-power adjustments, client distribution, and roaming tests.

Then we recreated the problem.

Twenty employees joined simultaneous video sessions. Several designers synchronized project files. Guest devices connected in the presentation room. Staff walked between the meeting suite and open workspace while staying on calls.

A single laptop did not suddenly display a spectacular speed-test number.

That was not the result we were looking for.

The network remained noticeably more consistent when many devices were active together. Video calls stayed stable, page loads became less erratic, and devices moved between zones with fewer interruptions.

The pilot continued for four weeks.

Management then approved a phased upgrade across the remaining high-density areas rather than replacing every lightly used office immediately. The final first-stage deployment included 18 WiFi 6 APs, managed PoE switching, and centralized management.

COMFAST also provided remote technical training for the integrator and our IT administrator. They reviewed client load, AP status, roaming behaviour, channel use, and configuration backup.

Six months later, employee numbers had increased again.

The office did not require another redesign.

That was the point.

WiFi 6 had been worth the investment not because “6” was larger than “5,” but because our workplace had changed from a collection of desks into a dense environment of cloud applications, video meetings, mobile users, guests, and multiple devices per person.

If an office remains lightly used and the existing WLAN is stable, replacing everything merely because a newer standard exists is difficult to justify.

Our office had already crossed that line.
