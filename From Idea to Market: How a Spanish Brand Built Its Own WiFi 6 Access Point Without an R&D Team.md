When we first discussed launching our own WiFi 6 access point brand in Spain, the conversation quickly centered around one key question: should we choose OEM or ODM? At a surface level, the difference seemed simple. OEM sounded like full control over product design, while ODM sounded like a faster way to market. But once we moved from theory to actual product planning, the distinction became much more strategic than expected.

Our company had been operating as a networking distributor for many years, focusing on importing and selling routers and access points from global manufacturers. However, with the rapid adoption of WiFi 6 in Europe, customer expectations started to shift. Hotels, SME installers, and system integrators increasingly asked for private-label networking solutions rather than generic branded products. They wanted consistency, stable firmware behavior, and predictable long-term supply.

Initially, OEM appeared to be the “ideal” path. In an OEM model, we would be responsible for defining everything from RF design and PCB layout to enclosure structure and firmware logic. The manufacturer would only act as a production partner. On paper, this offered full control and maximum branding freedom. However, the reality inside our company was different. We had no RF engineering team, no hardware design department, and no firmware development capability. Building such a structure would require years of investment and was not aligned with our goal of fast market entry.

At this stage, understanding the [OEM vs ODM WiFi manufacturing model](https://wifioem.com/oem-vs-odm-wireless-access-point/)  became a turning point in our decision-making process. It was no longer just a theoretical comparison—it directly influenced whether we could become a product brand owner in the WiFi hardware market or remain a traditional distributor.

After evaluating several options, we decided to adopt an ODM approach based on a COMFAST WiFi 6 access point platform. Instead of building everything from scratch, we started from a validated hardware architecture that had already been tested in enterprise environments. This allowed us to focus on product identity, firmware behavior, and market positioning rather than low-level engineering development.

The base platform we selected was an enterprise-grade WiFi 6 access point design. The RF performance, hardware stability, and core architecture were already proven in real-world deployments. Our role shifted toward customization at the product layer. We worked on Spanish firmware localization, simplified installation processes, multi-SSID configuration presets for hospitality use cases, and branding integration across the user interface.

We began with a pilot batch of 50 units. These were deployed in real environments, including boutique hotels and co-working spaces. The feedback from installers and users was very practical. Installation time was significantly reduced compared to traditional enterprise AP systems. Roaming performance across rooms and common areas was stable, and the simplified configuration process reduced setup errors for non-technical staff.

After collecting field feedback and making firmware adjustments, we moved into mass production with an order of 3,000 units. Compared to traditional OEM development cycles in the networking industry—which often require 8 to 12 months due to RF design, PCB development, enclosure tooling, firmware engineering, and certification testing—the ODM approach allowed us to reach production readiness in approximately 10 to 11 weeks.

COMFAST also supported us with CE certification documentation, RF testing preparation, and deployment validation guidance. This reduced regulatory uncertainty during European market entry and helped avoid delays that often occur in hardware certification stages.

From a business perspective, the outcome was immediate. The WiFi 6 access point line was positioned not as a generic imported device, but as a branded networking solution tailored for local installers and hospitality customers. This improved our market positioning and allowed us to build stronger relationships with system integrators. The first production batch sold out within a few weeks, mainly driven by hotel deployments and SME networking projects.

Looking back, the most important realization was that OEM and ODM are not simply manufacturing terms. They represent two fundamentally different business strategies. OEM is about engineering ownership, deep control, and long-term product development. ODM is about speed, resource efficiency, and leveraging existing technology platforms to accelerate market entry.

For companies without internal R&D capabilities, ODM is not just an alternative—it is often the most practical and realistic path to entering the WiFi access point market as a true brand owner.
