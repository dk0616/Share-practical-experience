For a long time, the Polish smart home installer treated routers the way many small contractors do: one model for small homes, another for larger homes, and a short explanation about WiFi speed printed on the quotation sheet. It worked well enough when clients only needed phones, laptops, and one television connected. Then the houses changed. Doorbell cameras appeared near gates, NAS devices entered home offices, smart speakers moved into kitchens, and sensors quietly multiplied behind walls and under staircases. The installer’s team noticed that customer complaints rarely sounded technical. Clients did not say, “My router cannot manage concurrent IoT traffic.” They said the camera at the garage was late, the TV buffered at night, or the smart lights sometimes ignored commands.

In several homes, broadband speed tests looked normal, but the connected devices still behaved poorly. That forced the company to rethink how it recommended home routers. Instead of purchasing one low-cost router in large volume, the installer placed a first mixed order of 300 COMFAST units, including CF-WR632AX, CF-WR633AX V2, and CF-WR673BE. The idea was simple: match the router to the home, not to the price list. Smaller apartments could use CF-WR632AX, heavier smart home layouts could move to CF-WR633AX V2, and larger connected homes or long-term upgrade projects could use CF-WR673BE.

COMFAST engineers supported the channel partner with a practical selection table. It divided homes by floor area, wall structure, number of IoT devices, camera usage, NAS demand, and remote work needs. The training also explained why 2.4GHz planning mattered for smart sensors, why camera upload stability was different from ordinary browsing, and why separating IoT devices from the main network could reduce troubleshooting time later. This made [smart home router selection](https://wifioem.com/how-to-choose-the-best-router-for-smart-homes/) feel like part of the installation design rather than an accessory sale.

After several months, the installer reported faster project matching and fewer repeated service visits. Technicians could explain why one house needed a stronger model while another did not. Customers also accepted the recommendation more easily because it was based on visible home conditions, not abstract speed numbers. The lesson was plain: in smart homes, the best router is not always the fastest one on the shelf. It is the one that fits the layout, device count, upload needs, and future expansion plan.
