This anonymised composite case reflects the purchasing decisions faced by a UK networking distributor serving homes, small businesses, installers, and online retailers.

I was the product manager responsible for our mobile broadband category. At first, choosing 4G and 5G routers seemed straightforward. We compared LTE or 5G specifications, WiFi standards, Ethernet ports, antenna designs, and wholesale prices.

Then our customers began asking questions that did not fit neatly into a specification table.

A family in Yorkshire wanted a fixed 4G router because their wired broadband was unreliable. A small retailer in Birmingham needed backup connectivity for payment terminals. A construction contractor wanted temporary internet for site offices. An online seller wanted portable routers that could be sold under its own brand. Another customer simply wanted a 5G CPE for a flat where installing fixed broadband was inconvenient.

All of them asked for a mobile router.

They did not need the same product.

Our existing supplier had several popular retail models, but customization was limited. Firmware language, APN presets, packaging, and branding were largely fixed. That was acceptable for normal retail sales but inconvenient when our B2B customers began requesting regional versions and private-label products.

We therefore reviewed several [global 4G/5G mobile router brands ](https://wifioem.com/the-global-top-8-4g-5g-mobile-router-brands/) instead of selecting one product from one familiar catalogue.

Our evaluation had five parts.

First came product range. We wanted portable 4G devices, fixed indoor CPE, multi-port routers for small businesses, and a path toward 5G products. A supplier with one successful MiFi device might be suitable for e-commerce but less useful for project customers.

Second was real-world stability. We installed sample units in twelve locations across England. They included homes, two convenience stores, a temporary office, a small warehouse, and several staff properties. The test ran for six weeks.

We did not judge the devices by one speed test.

We checked whether connections recovered after a mobile-network interruption, whether the routers remained stable during long working days, how warm they became under continuous load, whether external antennas were practical, and whether ordinary users could change APN and WiFi settings without calling support.

Third came network compatibility. Our technical staff tested SIM cards from several UK mobile operators because an imported router that performs well in one market is not automatically suitable for another. Frequency-band configuration and regional versions had to be confirmed before purchasing.

Fourth was channel cooperation.

This became the decisive factor.

COMFAST was included in the evaluation because the cooperation model allowed us to begin with standard products and then discuss branding, packaging, firmware language, APN presets, and regional configuration for larger orders.

We started with 72 trial units divided between portable and fixed-location models rather than committing to one large shipment.

The fixed routers were tested in small shops and home-backup applications. Larger models were placed in locations with several wired and wireless devices. Portable units went to sales staff who travelled regularly and needed temporary internet.

COMFAST engineers worked with our technical team during the pilot. When one store reported unstable connectivity, the first assumption was hardware failure. The logs and SIM tests showed that the cellular signal at the original router position varied significantly during the day. Moving the router and adjusting the antenna position produced a more stable connection.

That was useful because the response was diagnostic rather than simply replacing equipment.

After the pilot, we placed an initial commercial order of 520 units across several product levels. Some remained COMFAST-branded for normal distribution, while a smaller private-label batch received customised packaging and predefined configuration requirements.

Our support team also received a technical handover covering SIM detection, APN settings, network-mode checks, antenna placement, configuration backup, and the difference between cellular-network problems and local WiFi problems.

Within the following months, our sales staff stopped recommending mobile routers purely by advertised speed.

They began asking where the router would be used, how many devices were connected, whether mobility mattered, what operator SIM would be used, and whether the customer required retail stock or long-term project customization.

That made our product range slightly more complicated.

It made our purchasing decisions considerably better.
