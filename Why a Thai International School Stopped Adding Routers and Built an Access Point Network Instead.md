For many schools, wireless problems begin with a familiar mistake: when one router cannot cover enough space, add another router. At first, the logic seems reasonable. A classroom has weak signal, so a router is placed nearby. The library has poor WiFi, so another router is installed. A laboratory needs wireless access, so a third router appears on a shelf. Over time, the school believes it has expanded its network, when in reality it has created several small networks that do not behave as one system.

A Thai international school faced this exact problem before upgrading its campus wireless infrastructure. The school had teaching buildings, a library, science laboratories, staff offices, and several shared corridors where teachers and students moved throughout the day. The original wireless setup relied on multiple ordinary routers placed in different areas. On paper, it looked like a practical solution. Each router provided WiFi nearby, and the school avoided the cost of a more structured system during the early stage.

The weakness became obvious as digital teaching increased. Teachers began moving between classrooms with tablets and laptops. Students used online learning platforms more often. The library became a study area where many devices connected at the same time. Laboratory lessons required stable access for teaching materials and experiment records. Online exams created short periods of high network pressure. The router-based setup could not handle these changes smoothly.

The first visible problem was the number of network names. Different routers created different SSIDs, and users often had to reconnect manually when moving between rooms. Some devices stayed attached to a weak router even when a stronger signal was nearby. This caused slow access, dropped sessions, and frustration for teachers who were trying to conduct lessons without stopping to solve WiFi issues. During online exams, the situation became more serious because even a short disconnection could interrupt the test process.

The school initially considered buying stronger routers. This is another common reaction. Many organizations assume that a higher-priced router will solve a coverage problem. In small homes or very small offices, that may sometimes help. In a campus environment, however, the issue is usually not router power. The issue is network architecture.

After reviewing the campus layout, COMFAST engineers explained the difference between using several routers and building a managed AP system. The discussion around  [wireless router vs access point ](https://wifioem.com/wireless-router-vs-wireless-access-point/)   became central to the project. A router is designed to manage routing, NAT, DHCP, firewall functions, and WiFi for a relatively limited environment. An access point, by contrast, is designed to extend wireless coverage under a central network structure. When many areas must share one organized wireless system, AP deployment is usually more suitable.

The school selected a COMFAST solution using E395AX access points, SG181P PoE switches, and an AC100 gateway. The final deployment included 42 E395AX units, 5 SG181P PoE switches, and 1 AC100 gateway. Instead of placing equipment randomly, the campus was divided into functional zones: classrooms, library, laboratories, staff offices, and corridor transition areas. Each zone was planned according to device density, movement patterns, and teaching requirements.

PoE deployment also simplified installation and maintenance. Instead of needing power adapters near every AP location, the SG181P switches provided centralized power and data delivery. This helped keep classrooms cleaner and made future maintenance more predictable. The AC100 gateway supported centralized management, allowing the school IT team to monitor AP status and adjust settings more efficiently than before.

COMFAST engineers also provided training for the school’s technical staff. The training covered AP grouping, SSID planning, roaming behavior, exam network optimization, and basic troubleshooting. This was important because the school did not want a system that only external technicians could understand. The internal IT team needed to maintain the network during ordinary teaching days and major academic events.

After deployment, the school reported a clear improvement in daily use. Teachers could move between rooms with fewer connection interruptions. Students in the library experienced more stable access during study periods. Laboratory areas became easier to manage. Online exams were less affected by roaming problems and inconsistent router behavior. Most importantly, the IT team could diagnose network issues by AP zone instead of guessing which independent router was causing the problem.

The lesson from this Thai school project is straightforward. A router is useful when the environment is small, the number of users is limited, and the network does not need much expansion. An access point network becomes more suitable when the area is larger, users move frequently, and many devices must share a consistent wireless experience.

The school did not simply need stronger WiFi. It needed a network designed for how teachers and students actually used the campus. That is why adding more routers was not the answer. Building a managed AP system was.
