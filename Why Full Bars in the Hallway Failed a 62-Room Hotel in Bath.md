This is an anonymised composite case based on a typical UK hotel retrofit.

The building had been a row of Georgian townhouses before it became a 62-room boutique hotel in Bath. Its guests saw polished stone, tall windows, heavy fire doors, narrow staircases, and bedrooms that looked as though they had always belonged together. The network engineer saw something else: three joined buildings, five floors, several wall thicknesses, two old service shafts, one modern lift core, and bathrooms placed directly between the corridor and the beds.

The hotel believed its WiFi coverage was complete. A phone showed strong signal in most corridors. The installer could walk each floor without losing the SSID. Yet the complaint log named the same rooms repeatedly.

Guests in corner rooms lost stable video calls at the desk. In several premium rooms, phones worked near the door but weakened beside the bed. Smart TVs buffered after the bathroom door was closed. Rooms beside the lift behaved differently from rooms only a few metres away.

The hotel’s engineering supervisor did not begin by requesting more access points. He asked the front desk to record the room number, device type, time, and exact place where each problem occurred. The team then tested like guests: the room door closed, a laptop on the desk, a phone beside the bed, and the television streaming.

That changed the floor plan. The corridors still looked healthy, but the rooms did not.

The UK integrator sent the drawings and test notes to COMFAST. Engineers divided the property into four room types: standard rooms facing the street, inward-facing rooms behind thick bathroom blocks, corner rooms with longer signal paths, and larger suites created where two original rooms had been combined. They also marked mirrors, fire doors, risers, the lift core, cable routes, and the positions of the existing switches.

This was the principle behind  [room-by-room hotel WiFi design](https://wifioem.com/how-to-design-hotel-wifi-coverage-that-reaches-every-room/) : the hotel was not selling signal in a passageway; it was promising usable connectivity where a guest worked, slept, and watched television.

The first proposal did not copy one access-point pattern across all five floors. Standard sections used COMFAST ceiling-mounted WiFi 6 access points. Difficult suites and rooms behind heavier partitions received COMFAST in-wall access points positioned closer to the actual device area. Managed PoE switches supported the new cable runs, while an AC gateway gave the engineering team one view of AP status and configuration.

Because the building was listed, drilling and new cable routes had to be limited. The integrator reused suitable pathways where possible, but the radio plan was not allowed to become a servant of the easiest cable route. Where an ideal ceiling position was impossible, engineers changed the AP type or shifted coverage responsibility between neighbouring rooms.

The hotel approved a pilot on the fourth floor, where complaints were most frequent. Four ceiling APs, three in-wall APs, and one PoE switch were installed. COMFAST engineers joined the integrator remotely to review channel use, transmit power, client association, and coverage with room doors closed.

The trial lasted twelve days. The team repeated tests at the desk, bed, and TV in every room. Two AP positions were moved before the result was accepted.

The final deployment used 16 ceiling access points, eight in-wall access points, two managed PoE switches, and one AC gateway. The hotel also received a practical handover session. Maintenance staff learned how to identify a single-room coverage issue, an offline AP, a PoE fault, or a wider network problem before sending a general complaint to the provider.

During the following fully booked weekend, the recurring room complaints fell from several per day to two minor device-specific reports. The troublesome corner rooms maintained stable calls, and the premium suites no longer depended on signal entering through the doorway.

The important result was not the number of access points. It was that every device had a reason for being where it was.
