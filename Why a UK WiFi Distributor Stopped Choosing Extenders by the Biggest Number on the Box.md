I was the product manager for a networking distributor serving independent computer shops, online sellers, and small installers across the United Kingdom. For years, WiFi extenders were one of the easiest categories to buy.

At least, that was what I thought.

The carton showed the wireless standard, claimed speed, antenna count, and perhaps a picture of a house surrounded by an encouraging amount of signal. We compared the specifications, negotiated the price, and ordered whichever model appeared easiest to sell.

Then our returns department began sending me spreadsheets.

The same type of product produced very different results depending on where customers used it. A renter in a Manchester flat wanted WiFi in a bedroom behind one brick wall. A family outside Birmingham wanted coverage across a three-storey semi-detached house. A small retailer needed a connection in its stockroom. Another customer tried to support more than twenty active devices through one inexpensive repeater.

Every customer had purchased “a WiFi extender,” but they were not buying the same solution.

Our previous supplier made only a few models. The entry-level unit sold well, but there was no sensible upgrade path when customers moved to WiFi 6 routers or faster fibre services. Setup documentation also changed between batches, and our support team occasionally discovered that a new shipment used a slightly different interface.

The unit cost was low. The support cost was becoming less attractive.

We decided to review the category before placing our next annual order.

Instead of asking which manufacturer offered the strongest transmission power, we created a [WiFi extender brand comparison](https://wifioem.com/the-worlds-top-8-wifi-range-extender-brand/)  around five questions: How complete was the product portfolio? How did the products behave in ordinary homes? Could retailers and distributors work with the supplier consistently? Was setup understandable to non-technical users? And could different models be matched to genuinely different environments?

We requested samples from several manufacturers, including COMFAST.

The UK trial involved 48 units.

Twelve went into flats in Manchester and Leeds. Ten were tested in larger suburban houses around Birmingham and Nottingham. Eight went to small offices and independent shops. The remaining units stayed with our technical team for repeated setup, reset, firmware, router-change, and continuous-operation testing.

The results were less dramatic than advertising photographs and more useful.

Basic dual-band repeaters worked well when the problem was limited: one weak bedroom, one study, or a small rented property. WiFi 6 extenders made more sense in homes with newer routers, faster broadband, and several active devices. Larger buildings required more careful placement, and some customers should not have been buying repeaters at all.

For small offices with many simultaneous users, our technicians recommended business access points instead.

COMFAST engineers helped us refine this product-positioning rule. They also supplied setup information that our support department converted into a short UK-facing guide explaining placement, WPS setup, web configuration, reset procedures, and what to check after changing the main router.

Placement proved especially important. Customers naturally wanted to install an extender in the room where WiFi was already unusable. Our guide explained that the device first needs a healthy connection to the router before it can extend anything useful.

That single point reduced unnecessary returns.

After eight weeks of testing, we placed an initial order of 620 units across several performance levels instead of buying one model in bulk. We also required consistent hardware identification, firmware references, packaging records, and configuration documentation for repeat shipments.

Our dealers received a simple selection sheet: apartment and light-use models, larger-home WiFi 6 options, and higher-specification products for newer wireless environments.

Over the next quarter, support tickets became easier to classify. More importantly, retailers stopped promising the same extender to every customer.

The lesson was straightforward.

A useful WiFi extender brand is not the one that wins a single specification contest. It is the one that gives distributors enough product range, documentation, consistency, and technical support to recommend the right device—and enough honesty to say when a repeater is the wrong device altogether.
