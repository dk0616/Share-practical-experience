A public tourist park in northern Thailand rarely looked crowded in photographs taken during ordinary weekdays. The real pressure arrived during long weekends and seasonal holidays, when hundreds of visitors entered the property within short periods of time. Families purchased tickets through mobile payment systems, tour guides uploaded videos from scenic walkways, security cameras streamed continuously toward the control office, and parking attendants relied on handheld terminals connected through the same outdoor wireless infrastructure.

The park’s previous network was not completely unusable. Signal coverage still existed along the entrance zone and pedestrian walkways. Yet the IT supervisor noticed that the network became unpredictable whenever visitor density increased. Mobile payments occasionally slowed down near the entrance gates, camera synchronization lagged during evening peaks, and tourists often complained that the public WiFi appeared connected but responded poorly once they moved deeper into the park.

The management team originally planned to expand the existing WiFi 6 infrastructure by simply adding more access points. However, the engineering contractor responsible for the project argued that the problem was no longer simple coverage. The network had entered a stage where capacity planning mattered more than signal strength.

That conversation eventually pushed the park toward a phased WiFi 7 deployment using COMFAST WA971 and WA973 outdoor access points.

The project began with a site survey focused less on geometric coverage maps and more on visitor movement behavior. Engineers observed how tourists gathered near ticket counters, paused near scenic photo areas, and concentrated around shuttle pickup points during the hottest hours of the afternoon. Those observations influenced AP placement more heavily than theoretical coverage diagrams.

WA971 units were installed along directional walkways where stable linear coverage mattered most. Engineers adjusted antenna direction carefully to reduce dead zones along the pedestrian routes connecting parking areas and entrance gates. Meanwhile, WA973 units were deployed near the ticket plaza and main gathering areas where heavier traffic density required greater wireless capacity and stronger environmental tolerance.

The final deployment included 18 WA971 units and 16 WA973 units across the park.

The engineering team also redesigned traffic separation inside the wireless system. Visitor WiFi traffic was isolated from payment terminals, security cameras, and internal management devices. According to the park’s IT supervisor, this segmentation became one of the most important improvements during high-density periods.

A local maintenance technician later explained that older outdoor projects often treated all wireless traffic as if every device behaved similarly. In reality, visitor phones, surveillance systems, and payment terminals create very different traffic patterns. Once all of them compete during holiday peaks, instability becomes much more visible.

COMFAST engineers provided remote planning assistance throughout the project, including guidance for directional coverage alignment, PoE budgeting, mounting height recommendations, and controller configuration training for the local IT staff. Because parts of the tourist park were difficult to access during business hours, the engineering team also helped schedule phased installation windows to avoid interrupting visitor operations.

Several months after deployment, the park management noticed measurable operational changes. Entrance payment systems remained more stable during holiday peaks, visitor login times improved near crowded zones, and security staff reported fewer interruptions while monitoring outdoor cameras.

Interestingly, the IT supervisor did not describe the upgrade primarily as a speed improvement. Instead, he described it as preparation for future growth.

Tourist parks rarely become less connected over time. More visitors carry multiple devices, more services move toward cloud synchronization, and more operational systems depend on wireless infrastructure every year. The management team concluded that postponing network modernization would likely create larger retrofit costs later.

One internal planning document even referenced an [outdoor WiFi 7 deployment](https://wifioem.com/wifi-7-access-point-deployment-guide) strategy designed specifically for environments where seasonal crowd density changes rapidly throughout the year.

The Thai park project ultimately reflected a practical lesson that many outdoor operators are beginning to recognize. Wireless infrastructure planning is no longer only about present coverage requirements. In growing public environments, the larger challenge often involves deciding how much future pressure the network must quietly absorb several years from now.
