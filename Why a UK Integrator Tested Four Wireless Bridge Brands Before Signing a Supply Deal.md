I was responsible for procurement at a systems integration company in Birmingham. We installed CCTV, access control, and wireless links for warehouses, farms, industrial yards, and small commercial estates across the UK.

Wireless bridges were never our largest purchase category, but they caused some of our most expensive mistakes.

A bridge might cost only a fraction of the entire project. Yet when one failed on a pole eight metres above a warehouse yard, the replacement required two technicians, a lifting platform, site approval, and sometimes several hours of travel.

For that reason, the cheapest unit was rarely the cheapest problem.

We had previously purchased wireless bridges from whichever distributor had stock. Our engineers therefore carried products from several brands, each with a different interface, mounting method, PoE arrangement, and configuration procedure.

That became difficult when we won a contract covering nine commercial sites in the Midlands.

The project required 26 wireless links. Some connected two CCTV poles over 180 metres. Others carried cameras, access control, and local WiFi between warehouses separated by 600 to 900 metres. Two sites needed links beyond one kilometre.

Instead of selecting a product from our existing stock, we compared four manufacturers.

Our first evaluation looked predictable: wireless standard, Ethernet ports, antenna specifications, claimed distance, enclosure rating, management functions, and price.

Then our engineering manager added another column: “Cost of returning to site.”

That changed the exercise.

A useful [wireless bridge brand comparison](https://wifioem.com/the-global-top-8-wireless-bridge-brands/)  must consider more than the highest speed printed on a specification sheet. Product range matters because a short CCTV link and a longer high-traffic backhaul should not automatically use the same hardware. Documentation matters because field technicians need repeatable installation procedures. Supply stability matters because projects are often expanded months later.

We requested sample units from several suppliers, including COMFAST.

The test involved eight links at two working sites.

At the first warehouse, we installed short links carrying four to six CCTV cameras. At the second site, longer links carried cameras, access-control traffic, and a remote office connection.

COMFAST supplied several bridge classes, allowing our engineers to match equipment to the actual role rather than using the largest model everywhere.

We tested the devices for thirty days.

Our technicians deliberately interrupted power, changed channels, restored saved configurations, replaced PoE components, and monitored links during rain and high traffic periods. We also asked a technician who had not configured the original installation to replace one unit using only our documentation.

That replacement exercise revealed more than another speed test.

A technically capable bridge is less useful to an integrator if only the engineer who installed it understands the configuration.

COMFAST engineers supported the pilot remotely. They reviewed mounting height, alignment, channel selection, PoE arrangements, and configuration backup. On one 780-metre link, throughput fell during certain afternoon periods. The radios were operating correctly; local interference had increased on the original channel. After reviewing the spectrum conditions and changing the channel plan, the link returned to its expected operating range.

The pilot also affected our purchasing decision in another way.

We asked each supplier what would happen six months later if we reordered the same model. We wanted hardware revisions and firmware changes to remain traceable. COMFAST provided model and revision information that could be incorporated into our internal project records.

After the pilot, we placed an initial order for 96 bridge units, plus six prepared spares, PoE accessories, mounting kits, and surge-protection components.

COMFAST also provided remote technical training for our purchasing, engineering, and field teams. The session covered model selection, link planning, configuration recovery, interference checks, and fault isolation.

Over the following six months, the equipment was deployed across warehouses, farms, and commercial yards. More importantly, our technicians stopped treating every bridge installation as a new product-learning exercise.

We had started by asking which global wireless bridge brand had the strongest specification.

We ended by asking a more useful question: which manufacturer made repeated projects easier to quote, install, support, and expand?
