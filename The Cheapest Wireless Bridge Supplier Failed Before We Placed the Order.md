This anonymised composite case reflects the supplier-selection problems faced by a security system integrator in Singapore.

I was responsible for procurement at a systems integration company working on an industrial estate in Jurong. The customer needed wireless links between a central security room, two warehouses, three gatehouses, a loading yard, and several remote CCTV poles.

The first phase required eighteen point-to-point links. More links would follow if the pilot performed well.

We requested quotations from three wireless bridge manufacturers. Their catalogues looked remarkably similar. Each supplier mentioned long range, outdoor housings, PoE, OEM services, stable performance, and technical support.

The lowest quotation was about 11 percent below the highest.

Our sales manager liked it. Our engineering manager did not.

The cheapest supplier recommended one bridge model for every link, although the applications were different. A gatehouse with two cameras was treated the same as a warehouse connection carrying twelve cameras, access-control traffic, and a local WiFi access point. When we asked how the products should be divided by application, the answer returned to maximum distance.

That was the first warning.

The second appeared when we requested samples. The sample label did not match the model description in the quotation. The supplier explained that the internal board had recently changed, but could not provide a clear revision comparison. The PoE injector in the sample box also differed from the photograph in the manual.

None of these details proved that the bridge could not work. They showed that repeat procurement could become difficult.

Our project required configuration records, spare-unit preparation, installation guidance, and consistent replacements. A bridge that changes without clear documentation can create confusion long after its purchase price has been forgotten.

We therefore evaluated manufacturers through  [a reliable bridge supplier checklist ](https://wifioem.com/choosing-a-reliable-wireless-bridge-manufacturer-12-things-buyers-must-know/) rather than a specification table alone.

COMFAST engineers reviewed our site drawings and divided the links by role. Short gatehouse and light camera connections were matched with CF-E312A V2 units. Standard security links with higher traffic were assigned CF-E115A bridge pairs. The more difficult warehouse and loading-yard paths used CF-E319A V3 units because the remote networks were larger and the support consequence of failure was higher.

The recommendation included mounting height, line-of-sight notes, PoE arrangements, surge protection, outdoor cable handling, and spare-unit planning. More importantly, the engineers explained why each model belonged in each role.

We ordered six bridge pairs for a pilot instead of releasing the complete purchase order.

The pilot covered two gatehouses, one warehouse, a loading-yard camera cluster, and two remote poles. Our technicians installed the units using a shared configuration template. COMFAST provided model-specific manuals, a revision list, wiring guidance, and remote commissioning support.

One link became unstable during the second week. A less useful supplier might have recommended replacing both radios. COMFAST engineers reviewed the signal readings, channel information, photographs, and PoE data. The bridge was operating normally. Water had entered an improperly sealed outdoor connector after heavy rain.

The cable termination was replaced and the installation method was corrected across the remaining sites.

That incident influenced our final decision more than a successful bench test would have. The manufacturer did not simply defend the product or suggest random replacement. The support team helped isolate the actual fault.

After the thirty-day pilot, we ordered thirty-six additional bridge units, four prepared spares, managed PoE switches, mounting accessories, and surge-protection components. COMFAST also supported customised labels and packaging for our integrator brand while keeping the original model and revision information traceable.

Before shipment, we received the confirmed hardware revision, firmware version, packing list, and configuration references. Our field team then attended a remote technical session covering alignment, configuration backup, PoE diagnosis, interference checks, and replacement procedures.

During the first six months, the system remained stable. Two support cases were reported: one damaged Ethernet cable and one camera-side switch fault. Neither required bridge replacement.

The lowest-cost supplier might also have delivered working hardware. The problem was that we could not predict what would happen after the first order.

We chose the manufacturer that left fewer unanswered questions for engineering, procurement, and future maintenance.
