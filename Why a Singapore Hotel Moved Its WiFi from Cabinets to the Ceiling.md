This anonymised composite case reflects the conditions of a typical business hotel WiFi renovation in Singapore.

I was the engineering manager of a 126-room business hotel near Lavender. The property had eight guest floors, two meeting rooms, a breakfast restaurant, a lobby lounge, and a small co-working area used heavily by business travellers.

Our internet connection was not slow. Speed tests beside the reception desk regularly produced respectable results. Yet guests reported frozen calls, weak signals beside their beds, and interruptions when moving from their rooms to the lift lobby.

The complaints followed a pattern.

Several access points had been installed inside corridor cabinets because the cable routes were convenient. Others were fixed low on walls near housekeeping storage rooms. Two consumer-style routers had been added to the meeting area after an earlier event produced complaints.

The devices were working, but their positions were not.

Signals from the cabinet-mounted units had to pass through metal doors, concrete walls, bathroom partitions, mirrors, and room furniture. The wall-mounted units covered the nearest doorway well but produced uneven results farther inside the rooms. Housekeeping trolleys and stored linen occasionally blocked the lowest devices.

Our maintenance team first suggested adding more access points. The local system integrator asked us to test the existing layout before buying anything.

For seven days, hotel staff recorded the room number, complaint time, device type, and guest location. Engineers then tested each floor with room doors closed. They checked the entrance, desk, bed, television area, and bathroom doorway. They also observed roaming between guest corridors, lift lobbies, the breakfast restaurant, and meeting rooms.

The survey showed that the main problem was not the number of radios. It was inconsistent placement.

COMFAST engineers reviewed the floor plans, ceiling routes, wall structure, room clusters, and existing switch locations. They proposed a  [ceiling AP deployment strategy](https://wifioem.com/why-hotels-prefer-ceiling-access-point-deployment-for-reliable-wifi/)  based on repeatable coverage zones rather than placing devices wherever a power outlet happened to exist.

The hotel approved a pilot on the sixth floor, where business travellers had reported the most video-call interruptions. Six COMFAST WiFi 6 ceiling access points replaced eight mixed devices. The units were connected through a managed PoE switch, allowing power and data to use the same structured cable.

Ceiling placement gave the radios a clearer path across the corridor and into both sides of the room cluster. It also removed loose adapters, visible wiring, and equipment that staff or guests could move accidentally.

COMFAST engineers supported the integrator remotely during commissioning. They reviewed AP spacing, channel allocation, transmit power, roaming settings, and client distribution. After the first room test, one AP was moved several metres because a bathroom block weakened coverage in two corner rooms.

The pilot ran for sixteen days. During that period, the sixth floor reached full occupancy twice. Video calls remained stable in the rooms that had previously generated repeated complaints, and devices moved more consistently between the corridor and lift lobby.

The final project included 34 ceiling access points, three managed PoE switches, and one AC gateway for central configuration and monitoring. Additional ceiling APs were placed in the breakfast restaurant, lobby, meeting rooms, and co-working area because these spaces experienced concentrated traffic at different times of day.

Guest, staff, and hotel-operation traffic were separated. Housekeeping tablets and maintenance devices received their own network policy instead of sharing an uncontrolled guest connection.

COMFAST later conducted a remote training session for the hotel engineer, duty managers, and local installation team. The session covered AP status checks, PoE fault isolation, client-load review, configuration backup, and the difference between a room-level coverage issue and a building-wide internet problem.

During the following corporate-event week, occupancy reached approximately 96 percent. More than 500 guest and operational devices connected during the busiest evening. The hotel recorded three minor device-specific reports, compared with several recurring WiFi complaints each day before the renovation.

Ceiling access points were not selected because ceilings make equipment more advanced. They were selected because the position was repeatable, discreet, easier to power, and better suited to the way guests moved through the property.
