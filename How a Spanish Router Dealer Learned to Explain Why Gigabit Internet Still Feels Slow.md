A router dealer in Spain began receiving the same complaint from apartment users again and again: “I bought gigabit internet, but my WiFi only shows around 300Mbps.” At first, many customers assumed the broadband provider had failed to deliver the promised speed. The dealer’s support team understood why people felt that way. If a family pays for a gigabit plan, they expect the number on a speed test to look close to 1Gbps. But in many city apartments, the problem is not always the line from the operator. The slowest part is often inside the home.

The dealer worked mainly with small and medium-sized apartments in urban areas. Many users still had older AC routers that could connect to the internet without obvious failure. These routers were not broken in a simple sense. They could still browse websites, stream video, and support daily use. The problem appeared when customers expected gigabit-level WiFi across the apartment. In several homes, speed tests near the old router showed better results, while bedrooms, corners, or areas behind concrete walls dropped to 250–320Mbps. The router was working, but it was no longer suitable for the customer’s new broadband package.

To reduce repeated complaints, the dealer placed a first order of 180 COMFAST CF-WR632AX routers. COMFAST engineers provided remote training for the dealer’s sales and after-sales teams. The training focused on practical diagnosis rather than complicated terminology. The team learned to test by steps: wired connection first, close-range WiFi second, distant-room WiFi third, and then comparison between 2.4GHz and 5GHz. This helped the dealer explain the [gigabit internet speed issue](https://wifioem.com/why-is-my-gigabit-internet-only-300mbps/) more clearly instead of blaming the operator or replacing products blindly.

The CF-WR632AX was selected because it matched the needs of many small apartment upgrades. It provided WiFi 6 performance and better handling for modern devices, while remaining suitable for compact living spaces. COMFAST engineers also trained the dealer on router placement. Many users had placed old routers inside TV cabinets, behind furniture, or near electronic devices that caused interference. Once the router was moved to a more open location and the user connected through the correct band, the real experience improved noticeably.

The biggest change was not only speed. It was communication. Before the training, customers often saw “300Mbps” and immediately believed something was wrong with the broadband plan. After the dealer adopted the new testing process, support staff could show whether the bottleneck came from the router, distance, wall structure, device capability, or WiFi band. This reduced unnecessary arguments and made the after-sales process more efficient.

Several months after the first batch was installed, the dealer reported fewer complaints from small-apartment users. Customers near the router experienced much better wireless performance, and the support team could quickly separate broadband faults from home network limitations. The lesson was simple but useful: gigabit internet does not guarantee gigabit WiFi everywhere. The home network still needs the right router, the right placement, and a clear testing method.
