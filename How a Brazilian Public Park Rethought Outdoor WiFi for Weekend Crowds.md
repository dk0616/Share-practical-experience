In a Brazilian city park, the WiFi problem did not look serious on quiet weekdays. Visitors could sit near the lake, walk through the children’s area, or check messages near the entrance without much difficulty. The signal existed. The access points were working. The management office could still connect its mobile terminals. On paper, the network seemed acceptable.

The problem appeared on weekends.

By Saturday afternoon, the park changed completely. Families gathered around the children’s activity zone, runners used fitness apps near the outdoor gym area, visitors sat along the lake path uploading photos, and staff members used mobile devices for basic park operations. At the same time, security cameras continued sending video back to the management office. The old wireless network was not completely broken, but it became unpredictable when too many users and systems shared the same outdoor environment.

This is a common misunderstanding in public WiFi projects. A network can show signal and still fail under real load. Coverage is only one part of outdoor design. In a public park, user density, device movement, traffic separation, and operational systems often matter just as much as distance.

The Brazilian park management first considered a simple expansion. More access points seemed like the obvious answer. If one area was slow, add another device. If the lake path had complaints, add more coverage. If the children’s area became crowded, place another AP nearby. This thinking is understandable, but it can also create new problems. Too many poorly planned access points may increase interference, complicate maintenance, and still fail to solve the real bottleneck.

After reviewing the site, the project team chose a more structured approach. The park was divided into usage zones: entrance and service area, lake path, children’s activity zone, fitness area, rest spaces, and management points. Each zone had different behavior. Visitors did not use the network evenly. They gathered in waves, moved between attractions, and created temporary high-density areas during weekend afternoons.

COMFAST engineers helped the customer analyze these movement patterns and plan the AP locations accordingly. The final deployment used 28 COMFAST WA937 outdoor access points. The goal was not to cover every visible corner with hardware, but to place equipment where stable outdoor capacity was actually needed. The team also recommended separating visitor WiFi from management terminals and surveillance traffic, so that public usage would not interfere with operational systems.

This became the core of the park’s [outdoor WiFi deployment strategy](https://wifioem.com/hangzhou-west-lake-smart-scenic-area-outdoor-wifi-coverage-project) . The design treated the park as a living public space, not as a flat technical map. A lake path behaves differently from a ticket or service point. A children’s zone behaves differently from a quiet rest area. A camera network has different priorities from visitors browsing maps or uploading short videos.

Installation also required practical decisions. Outdoor APs had to be placed with weather exposure, power access, mounting height, and visual impact in mind. A city park is not an industrial yard. Equipment should support the visitor experience without making the environment feel cluttered. COMFAST provided remote technical guidance on AP positioning, coverage overlap, network separation, and basic maintenance training for the local technical team.

After deployment, the park reported more stable network performance during weekends. Visitors experienced smoother access for basic browsing, QR code use, and information lookup. Security camera connections became easier to manage, and park staff could use mobile terminals with fewer interruptions. The maintenance team also benefited from clearer network segmentation, because problems could be traced by zone and device type instead of being treated as one large unknown failure.

The most important lesson from this case is simple: public park WiFi should not be planned only around maximum coverage distance. A good outdoor network must reflect how people actually use the space. Weekend crowds, moving visitors, management terminals, cameras, and digital services all place different demands on the same infrastructure.

For parks, scenic areas, and outdoor public spaces, WiFi has become part of basic service infrastructure. It supports visitors, staff, security, and future digital management. But it works well only when the design begins with real usage, not just hardware quantity.

The Brazilian park did not need a louder network. It needed a better organized one.
