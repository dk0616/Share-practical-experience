When people ask me what caused the biggest problem during our university's wireless network upgrade, they often expect me to mention unstable hardware, poor signal strength, or software bugs. Surprisingly, none of those were the real issue. The biggest mistake happened long before the first access point was installed. It happened during procurement, when we treated enterprise wireless infrastructure like ordinary office equipment instead of a long-term network system.

I work as a network engineer at a comprehensive university in Thailand with more than 12,000 students. Our campus includes multiple teaching buildings, student dormitories, libraries, administration offices, cafeterias, outdoor study areas, and sports facilities. Over the past few years, online learning platforms, cloud-based teaching systems, and mobile applications have become an essential part of daily campus life. The existing wireless infrastructure could no longer support the growing number of connected devices, so the university approved a campus-wide WiFi upgrade.

The project looked relatively straightforward in the beginning. The procurement team organized a public bidding process and compared products based primarily on three factors: wireless standard, hardware specifications, and purchase price. On paper, the selected products appeared competitive. They supported modern WiFi standards, advertised high theoretical throughput, and came from multiple suppliers that offered attractive quotations. From a purchasing perspective, everything seemed reasonable.

Unfortunately, the procurement process overlooked something much more important. Nobody spent enough time buying wireless access points [buying wireless access points](https://wifioem.com/buying-wireless-access-points-mistakes/)  based on actual deployment requirements. Instead of evaluating how the devices would operate together across the campus, we evaluated them individually. The result was a network built from several different brands, management platforms, and firmware ecosystems.

At first, the problems were not immediately obvious. During initial testing, each access point functioned normally within its own coverage area. However, once thousands of students returned to campus, operational issues started appearing almost every day. Students walking from one classroom building to another frequently experienced interrupted connections because roaming between different wireless systems was inconsistent. In the library, hundreds of simultaneously connected devices caused unstable performance during peak study hours. Student dormitories developed coverage gaps because access point density had been calculated according to floor area rather than actual user density. Even routine maintenance became frustrating because our IT department had to manage multiple controller interfaces, separate firmware versions, and different configuration methods.

The hardware itself was not defective. The real problem was that the network had never been designed as a unified system.

After several months of troubleshooting, the university decided to stop expanding the existing deployment and instead redesign the project completely. This time, before discussing products or pricing, we invited COMFAST engineers to conduct a complete wireless site survey. Instead of immediately recommending equipment, they spent time understanding how students, teachers, and administrative staff actually used the campus. They analyzed classroom occupancy, library traffic, outdoor gathering areas, dormitory layouts, roaming paths between buildings, and the expected number of concurrent wireless devices during examination periods.

Based on this assessment, COMFAST proposed replacing the fragmented architecture with a unified enterprise wireless solution built around E395AX Ceiling Access Points, SG181P PoE switches, and the AC100 Controller. More importantly, they explained why network architecture mattered far more than individual product specifications. Rather than deploying access points wherever signal appeared weak, the design focused on consistent coverage, centralized management, seamless roaming, and future scalability.

The implementation was carried out in stages to avoid disrupting teaching activities. More than 320 ceiling access points were installed during the first phase, followed by another 180 units several months later as new academic buildings were completed. Every access point operated under the same centralized management platform, allowing the IT department to configure SSIDs, monitor device status, perform firmware updates, and diagnose network issues from a single interface. PoE switches simplified installation by delivering both power and network connectivity through one Ethernet cable, reducing construction complexity while making future maintenance much easier.

COMFAST engineers also organized several technical workshops for our network administrators. These sessions covered wireless heat-map analysis, roaming optimization, channel planning, access point grouping, controller management, and long-term maintenance strategies. Looking back, this training proved almost as valuable as the hardware itself because it changed how our team approached enterprise wireless networking.

The results became noticeable shortly after deployment. Students moving between lecture halls remained connected without repeated authentication. The library handled significantly higher client density during examination periods. Dormitory coverage became much more consistent, and outdoor study areas that previously relied on mobile data now provided stable wireless access. Most importantly, the IT department no longer spent hours switching between multiple management platforms to perform routine maintenance. Daily operations became simpler, faster, and far more predictable.

This project completely changed my understanding of enterprise wireless procurement. Before, I believed selecting access points meant comparing specifications, throughput, and pricing. Experience taught me that enterprise WiFi should never be purchased as isolated hardware. Every access point becomes part of a much larger network architecture involving roaming, controller management, PoE infrastructure, firmware consistency, scalability, and long-term technical support.

Today, whenever another university asks for advice about upgrading campus WiFi, I rarely begin by discussing wireless standards or product specifications. Instead, I tell them that the most expensive procurement mistake is not buying equipment that costs too much. It is purchasing devices without first understanding how the entire network will operate over the next five years. Once that planning is completed, choosing the right access point becomes a much easier decision.
