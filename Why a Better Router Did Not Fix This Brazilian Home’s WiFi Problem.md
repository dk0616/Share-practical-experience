A smart home consultant is often called after the homeowner has already tried the most obvious solution. In one Brazilian independent house project, the owner had upgraded the broadband plan, replaced the main router, and even tried a WiFi extender near the hallway. The living room looked fine during speed tests, but the front door camera still uploaded video unevenly, the backyard phones often switched to mobile data, and several smart devices near the garage corner occasionally dropped offline. The family believed the network was weak. The real issue was that the WiFi source was in the wrong place.

The house had a very common layout. The main router was placed in the living room because that was where the broadband line entered the home and where the TV cabinet already had power. For phones used on the sofa and the smart TV near the router, the network seemed acceptable. But the front door, backyard leisure area, and garage corner were all farther away, with walls, doors, and outdoor distance weakening the signal path. This is why the homeowner’s first upgrades did not fully solve the problem. A stronger router in the same poor location still had to push signal through the same difficult route.

At first, the family tried a WiFi extender because it appeared easy. It reduced the problem slightly in one indoor area, but the outdoor camera and backyard still remained inconsistent, especially during rainy periods or when several devices were active at the same time. This is a common limitation of quick repeater-style fixes. They may help with light browsing, but they do not always provide the stable connection needed by cameras, smart locks, or outdoor family spaces.

The solution was to treat the home as several zones rather than one single room with a router. COMFAST engineers suggested using two E393AX indoor access points, one EW74 outdoor access point, and one SG181P PoE switch. The indoor APs were positioned to improve coverage toward the hallway, garage-side room, and rear part of the house. The EW74 was used for the backyard and outdoor camera area, where weather resistance and better outdoor placement mattered. The PoE switch simplified cabling and power delivery, reducing the need for separate power adapters near each AP location.

This became a practical example of choosing the [best home wireless access point](https://wifioem.com/best-home-wireless-access-point/)  by location and usage, not by the largest speed number. The front door camera needed stable upload. The backyard needed usable WiFi for phones and tablets. The garage corner needed better reach for smart devices. Each zone had a different job, so one central router could not handle all of them equally well.

COMFAST engineers also provided guidance on AP placement, outdoor mounting, camera upload testing, and basic troubleshooting. The homeowner learned to test the network in the places that actually mattered: beside the front door camera, near the backyard table, inside the garage corner, and in the hallway between indoor and outdoor zones. This was more useful than only testing beside the router, where results had always looked good.

After deployment, the family saw the difference in daily use. The front door camera uploaded more consistently, backyard phones stayed on WiFi more often, and smart devices near the garage disconnected less frequently. The improvement did not come from making one router louder. It came from placing wireless access closer to the weak areas.

The lesson is simple. In many homes, WiFi problems are not caused by bad broadband or an underpowered router. They are caused by distance, walls, outdoor edges, and poor placement. When the same room always works and the same far areas always fail, the answer is often not another router. It is a better-positioned access point.
