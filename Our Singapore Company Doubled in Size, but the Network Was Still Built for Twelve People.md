I co-founded a commercial interior-design company in Singapore. When we moved into our first office in Ubi, there were twelve employees, one meeting room, a printer, and a router supplied by the internet provider.

It worked.

Four years later, we had thirty-eight employees, a design studio, two meeting rooms, a sample warehouse, cloud-based project software, IP cameras, visiting contractors, hybrid staff, and a small Johor-facing project team working remotely.

We still had essentially the same network.

We had added things, of course.

A WiFi extender appeared near the warehouse. Another router served the second meeting room. Someone created a guest password for clients. The finance team used cloud accounting. Designers uploaded large files. Remote staff connected to office resources. Cameras recorded continuously.

The network had grown the way old houses sometimes grow: one extension at a time, without anyone redrawing the original plan.

The first serious warning came on a Monday afternoon.

Two designers were uploading large render files. A client presentation was running in Meeting Room B. Finance was processing online payments. Three remote employees were connected. A supplier team was using guest WiFi.

The video call began breaking up.

Then the warehouse tablet lost its connection.

Someone restarted the router.

For six minutes, everything disappeared.

That afternoon convinced us to stop asking whether we needed a faster router.

A Singapore system integrator reviewed our office and sent the findings to COMFAST engineers. They looked at user count, device types, cloud traffic, WiFi coverage, remote access, branch plans, and what would happen if the main internet connection failed.

The discussion became an [SMB network upgrade planning]https://wifioem.com/7-smb-network-upgrade-problems-growing-businesses-cannot-ignore/)  exercise rather than a router replacement.

Seven weaknesses appeared.

The network had little room for more users. WiFi coverage depended on extenders. Guest and internal devices were not clearly separated. Remote work relied on an improvised VPN arrangement. There was no secondary internet path. The warehouse and office used inconsistent settings. Most importantly, one router failure could interrupt nearly every cloud-dependent activity at once.

We decided to upgrade in stages.

COMFAST WiFi 6 access points replaced the scattered router-and-extender arrangement. A managed PoE switch powered the access points and simplified installation. A business gateway handled traffic policies, while guest devices were separated from employee laptops, cameras, printers, and finance systems.

The integrator also configured a cellular backup connection for essential internet access. It was not designed to reproduce the full office experience during an outage. Its job was to keep critical cloud applications, payments, messaging, and remote coordination working.

Hybrid access was cleaned up as well. Only employees who required remote access received it, and they no longer depended on an administrator enabling ad hoc settings every time somebody travelled.

The pilot ran for three weeks.

The most useful test happened accidentally.

Construction work in the area interrupted the primary internet service for almost forty minutes. Staff noticed slower performance because the backup connection had less capacity, but finance remained online, messages continued, and the project team could access essential cloud systems.

Nobody restarted anything.

After the pilot, we completed the deployment across the office and warehouse. COMFAST engineers also provided remote technical guidance to the local integrator and our office administrator covering AP monitoring, PoE status, configuration backup, traffic separation, and basic fault isolation.

Six months later, we opened a small project office elsewhere in Singapore.

This time, nobody bought a random router on the way to the site.

The branch followed the same network structure, naming rules, security logic, and remote-access policy as headquarters.

Our original network had not failed because the first router was poor.

It failed because the business around it had changed.

Growing companies usually notice new employees, larger orders, and additional locations immediately. Network growth is quieter.

Until one Monday afternoon, when everyone notices it at once.
