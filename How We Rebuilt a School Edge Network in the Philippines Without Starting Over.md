I was brought into the project after everyone had already learned the wrong lesson.

The school in the Philippines did not believe it had an outdoor WiFi problem at first. It believed it had a few inconvenient spots. That is how these things usually begin. One complaint comes from the edge of the sports ground. Another comes from the covered walkway between two buildings. A teacher says the connection drops when moving outside after class. A staff member says the cameras are fine but student devices feel slow on activity days. None of those complaints sound large on their own, so nobody gives them the respect they deserve.

By the time I arrived, the private campus and training center had already spent months treating symptoms instead of the system. A few access points had been added where trouble looked worst. Some devices were repositioned. One area got better, another one became inconsistent. The result was familiar: more hardware, more guesswork, more small frustrations.

The site itself was not huge, but it was complicated in the way real outdoor spaces often are. Students moved constantly between the teaching block, the side corridor, and the sports ground. The edge zones of the campus were not empty. They held people waiting, walking, recording attendance, checking messages, watching training clips, and using learning platforms. On event days, the covered seating area near the field became far busier than the quiet map of the site would ever suggest.

That was the first important point: the campus did not need stronger signal everywhere in the abstract. It needed a network that behaved properly under movement, shared use, and repeated daily load.

The second point was that one model for the whole outdoor site would have been the wrong kind of simplicity. Some areas needed a balanced enterprise-grade WiFi 6 platform that could cover the daily flow well and remain manageable. Other areas, especially the denser points around the stands and the outer activity edge, needed a tougher tier with more headroom and stronger environmental tolerance.

That is why the final design used COMFAST WA933 for the mainstream outdoor zones and WA937 for the higher-pressure points.

That division helped because it matched the campus rather than forcing the campus to match the hardware. The WA933 units handled the outer corridors, general edge areas, and routine outdoor movement spaces where stable daily coverage and cleaner roaming mattered most. The WA937 units were assigned to the more demanding parts of the site, where heavier use, more exposure, and stricter performance expectations made a stronger outdoor platform the safer choice.

What mattered in practice was not the excitement of installation day. It was what happened two weeks later, when students returned to their ordinary habits and the campus resumed behaving like itself. Staff moved between zones without the usual awkward pauses. Student devices in the outdoor edge areas stayed more consistent. The IT team stopped treating every activity day like a small emergency. Even routine checks became easier, because the network no longer felt like a pile of separate outdoor fixes pretending to be a system.

COMFAST’s support was useful in a very practical way. The engineering discussion did not stay trapped at the level of brochure language. We talked about where users actually paused, where transitions happened, how the outdoor edges behaved during busier hours, and which points would become maintenance burdens if they were handled too casually. The school’s team also received guidance on placement logic, mixed-tier deployment, and how to think about roaming zones instead of only coverage circles.

That matters more than people admit. Outdoor projects often fail because the team buys a decent device and then treats deployment like a matter of filling blanks on a map. But school grounds are not blank maps. They are moving patterns. A network near a field behaves differently from a network beside a walkway. A shaded seating zone behaves differently from an open edge strip. When users move, the network must move politely with them.

If there is one lesson I would pass on to another engineer, it is this: do not judge an outdoor campus network by whether the signal reaches the far side. Judge it by whether the site remains calm when people start using it the way they actually live in it.

That is why I now tell teams to read about [enterprise outdoor WiFi planning](https://wifioem.com/outdoor-wifi-access-point-guide) before they start adding more boxes to poles. A tidy map is easy to build. A dependable outdoor system is something else.
