For a business hotel in São Paulo, WiFi complaints did not begin as a dramatic technical failure. The network was not completely down, and guests could usually connect somewhere in the building. The problem was more irritating than obvious: one room had strong signal while another room on the same floor struggled; guests leaving the lobby lost connection in the corridor; video meetings froze during busy hours; and the hotel IT team spent too much time answering the same question from different rooms—“Why is the WiFi unstable?”

The hotel had 84 rooms, plus a lobby, breakfast restaurant, meeting rooms, a small fitness area, and a shared business lounge. Its original network relied on several ordinary enterprise routers placed across different floors. This approach had worked when most guests used one phone and perhaps one laptop. But guest behavior changed. Business travelers now arrived with phones, laptops, tablets, smart watches, VPN tools, and cloud applications. During high-occupancy periods, the hotel could easily see more than 350 devices online at the same time.

The management team first considered adding more routers. It seemed like a natural answer. If one side of the corridor had weak signal, place another router nearby. If the meeting room became crowded, add another device. But after several internal tests, the hotel realized the problem was not only signal strength. The real issue was structure. Too many independent routers created uneven coverage, inconsistent roaming, and complicated maintenance. Guests did not want to choose between different WiFi names as they moved from lobby to room. The IT team did not want to log into separate devices whenever a problem appeared.

COMFAST engineers recommended a ceiling AP-based hotel WiFi design using 34 COMFAST E395AX ceiling access points, 5 SG181P PoE switches, and 1 AC100 gateway controller. The idea was simple: instead of placing routers on desks, shelves, or hidden corners, put wireless coverage where it could serve the space more evenly. This is where [ceiling access point deployment](https://wifioem.com/why-hotels-prefer-ceiling-access-point-deployment-for-reliable-wifi/)  made practical sense for the hotel.

The engineers first reviewed the floor layout, guest-room distribution, corridor length, and public-area density. The lobby and breakfast restaurant needed stronger capacity because many guests gathered there at the same time. The meeting rooms required stable connections for video calls and presentations. Guest-room corridors needed more even coverage so that rooms at the far ends of each floor would not suffer from weak signal. The goal was not to make every AP transmit at maximum power, but to create balanced coverage throughout the building.

PoE planning also became an important part of the project. With SG181P PoE switches, each ceiling AP could receive power and data through one Ethernet cable. This reduced visible wiring, avoided separate power adapters, and made the installation cleaner for a hotel environment. For a hospitality project, appearance matters. Guests should not see routers sitting in hallway corners or cables hanging near public areas. A ceiling AP can be installed neatly and quietly, supporting the network without disturbing the hotel’s interior design.

The AC100 gateway gave the hotel IT team a more organized management method. Instead of maintaining several routers separately, they could manage SSIDs, AP status, roaming behavior, and basic troubleshooting from a centralized structure. COMFAST also provided training for the local team, covering AP naming, floor-based maintenance records, signal testing, and guest network separation. This mattered because hotel IT staff must often handle many systems at once: PMS terminals, CCTV, office computers, guest WiFi, and meeting room support.

The project was completed in four days. After deployment, the difference was most visible in guest movement. A traveler could enter the lobby, walk through the corridor, and reach the room without repeatedly reconnecting to different networks. Meeting room users reported fewer interruptions during video calls. Rooms at the end of corridors had more consistent wireless access. The hotel also noticed fewer WiFi-related complaints at the front desk, especially from business guests who relied on VPN and cloud tools.

The lesson from this Brazilian hotel project is clear. Hotel WiFi should not be built like home WiFi. A hotel has many rooms, many guests, many devices, and many movement patterns. Ordinary routers may be enough for a small office or apartment, but they often become difficult to manage in hospitality environments. Ceiling access points, PoE switches, and centralized control create a cleaner and more maintainable structure.

The hotel manager later described the result in a practical way: the best WiFi is the one guests stop talking about. When the network works quietly in the background, guests focus on their stay, meetings, and travel—not on the signal icon.
