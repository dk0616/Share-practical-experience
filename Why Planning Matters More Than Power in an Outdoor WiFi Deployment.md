When people discuss outdoor wireless networks, the conversation often begins with hardware. Which access point has the highest transmit power? Which antenna provides the longest range? Which specification promises the fastest wireless speed? After working on several municipal wireless projects across Europe, I have learned that these questions are usually asked too early. Successful outdoor networking projects rarely begin with choosing a product. They begin with understanding how people actually use the space that needs to be connected.

One project that perfectly demonstrated this principle took place in a large public park in Poland. The municipal government had launched a smart-city initiative to modernize public infrastructure while improving digital services for residents and visitors. The park covered approximately seven hectares and included a central square, lakeside walking paths, children's playgrounds, cycling routes, outdoor fitness areas, a visitor information center, and an open-air event plaza where concerts and community activities were held throughout the year. During weekends and public holidays, daily visitor numbers frequently exceeded six thousand people. Public WiFi was no longer considered a convenience; it had become part of the city's public service strategy.

The original wireless infrastructure had been installed several years earlier and served only the visitor center and the park management office. Everywhere else, connectivity was inconsistent or unavailable. Visitors walking along the lakeside lost wireless access almost immediately after leaving the main building. Families attempting to upload photos or livestream local events often switched back to mobile data because the signal disappeared. Surveillance cameras positioned around entrances and pedestrian paths occasionally experienced unstable backhaul connections, making continuous monitoring difficult. Park maintenance staff relied on mobile networks while conducting inspections because the existing WiFi simply did not reach the areas where they spent most of their working day.

Initially, the city considered expanding the existing network by installing additional outdoor access points wherever complaints had been reported. On paper, this appeared to be the quickest solution. However, after conducting an on-site survey, COMFAST engineers suggested a completely different approach. Instead of asking where another access point should be mounted, they asked how people actually moved through the park. They studied pedestrian traffic, visitor gathering areas, tree density, building locations, lighting poles, power availability, surveillance camera positions, and seasonal activity patterns. The objective was not simply to extend signal farther. It was to build an [ceiling access point deployment](https://wifioem.com/outdoor-wifi-coverage-project-case)  that reflected how the park functioned every day.

Based on the survey, the engineering team designed a solution using twenty-four COMFAST EW74 Outdoor Access Points, four E395AX indoor access points for the visitor center and administration building, six SG181P PoE switches, and one AC100 Gateway Controller. Rather than concentrating equipment near existing buildings, wireless coverage was distributed across the entire park using strategically selected mounting locations. The lakeside paths received dedicated outdoor coverage because visitors frequently stopped there to rest, take photographs, and use mobile applications. The children's playground and fitness area were planned for higher user density, while the event plaza required additional capacity during concerts and public festivals. This planning process ensured that wireless resources matched actual visitor behavior instead of simply following available installation points.

Power distribution presented another important challenge. Running electrical infrastructure separately to every access point would have increased installation costs and complicated future maintenance. For that reason, the SG181P PoE switches became a key component of the project. Delivering both power and network connectivity through a single Ethernet cable reduced installation time, simplified cable management, and allowed equipment to be mounted on existing poles without additional electrical work. From a maintenance perspective, this approach also reduced the number of potential failure points while making future expansion much easier.

Centralized management was equally important. Managing dozens of outdoor devices individually would have created unnecessary operational complexity for the municipal IT department. The AC100 Gateway Controller allowed administrators to monitor equipment status, configure wireless parameters, manage SSIDs, perform firmware updates, and identify faults from a single interface. COMFAST engineers also provided technical training covering AP placement principles, roaming optimization, channel planning, power adjustment, and routine maintenance procedures. Instead of depending on external support for every small adjustment, the city's own technical team gained the confidence to maintain and expand the network independently.

The deployment was completed over five working days with minimal disruption to public access. Once the system entered operation, improvements became visible almost immediately. Visitors could remain connected while walking from the visitor center to the lakeside without repeatedly losing wireless access. Families using the playground experienced more reliable internet connections throughout their stay. Public events generated far fewer complaints about network congestion because user density had already been considered during the planning stage. Surveillance cameras maintained stable communication with the control center, improving overall security monitoring across the park. Maintenance personnel were finally able to access management applications while moving throughout the entire site instead of relying on mobile data.

Perhaps the most valuable lesson from the project was that outdoor wireless coverage should never be designed around equipment specifications alone. Parks, campuses, industrial sites, ports, and public squares all have unique movement patterns, physical obstacles, and operational requirements. A powerful access point installed in the wrong location rarely performs as well as a carefully planned network of properly positioned devices. Outdoor networking is ultimately an exercise in understanding people as much as understanding technology.

Today, the Polish park has become more than a recreational space. It is also a connected public environment supporting visitors, municipal staff, surveillance systems, and digital city services through one coordinated wireless infrastructure. Looking back, the success of the project was not determined by choosing the most expensive hardware available. It came from designing a network that reflected the environment it was built to serve.
