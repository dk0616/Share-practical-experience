This anonymised composite case is based on the kind of outdoor network decisions system integrators face in Singapore.

I was the project engineer for a network integrator asked to connect three detached areas at a horticultural supply yard in Kranji. Across the property sat a gatehouse, a packing shed, and a maintenance workshop. The customer wanted all three locations to behave like normal parts of the same business network without cutting through active loading lanes.

At first, the job looked like a simple distance exercise. The gatehouse was 170 metres away, the packing shed 410 metres, and the workshop about 760 metres from the office.

The endpoint roles made that approach careless.

The gatehouse carried four cameras and access-control events. The packing shed used two desktop terminals, a label printer, handheld scanners, and a local access point. The workshop carried eight cameras, two computers, maintenance tablets, and a planned group of environmental sensors. A failure at the gate was inconvenient. A failure at the workshop stopped maintenance records and removed a large section of CCTV coverage.

Site conditions were equally uneven. The gatehouse had clean line of sight. The packing-shed path crossed a service lane but remained open above vehicle height. The workshop route passed near tree canopy, metal storage racks, and moving equipment. All equipment would face heat, humidity, and heavy rain.

This was where a proper  [CPE bridge buying guide](https://wifioem.com/cpe-bridge-buying-guide/)  became more useful than the largest range number on a product page. We needed to compare endpoint workload, path quality, mounting height, operating frequency, Ethernet capacity, PoE arrangements, weather protection, and the cost of a failed link.

We sent COMFAST the site map, path photographs, camera count, estimated traffic, proposed pole heights, power locations, and expansion plan. Their engineers agreed that using one model everywhere would either overspend on the easy gatehouse or leave too little margin at the workshop.

For the gatehouse, we selected a lighter COMFAST CF-E115A bridge pair. The path was short and clear, and the remote workload was limited. The packing shed received a CF-E312A V2 pair because the far side behaved like a small occupied network rather than a single remote device. For the workshop, we used a CF-E319A V3 pair and raised both mounting points to protect the path from tree growth and stacked materials.

The decision was not based on distance alone. The strongest bridge went to the endpoint with the most demanding traffic and the highest operational cost of failure.

The customer approved a staged pilot. We installed the packing-shed link first because it represented the middle case: more traffic than the gatehouse, but fewer path complications than the workshop. COMFAST engineers joined the commissioning remotely and reviewed bridge mode, channel choice, alignment, signal quality, management access, and configuration backup.

The pilot ran for twenty-one days. During that period, Singapore supplied several thunderstorms and enough rain to reveal weak cable sealing. One outdoor connector had been terminated correctly but protected poorly. We corrected the weatherproofing before the full deployment, which was precisely why the pilot mattered.

The final purchase included three matched bridge pairs, one spare unit for each model, three managed PoE switches, surge-protection devices, mounting hardware, outdoor-rated cable, and sealed junction boxes.

COMFAST later provided remote training for our engineers and the customer’s maintenance supervisor. The session covered alignment checks, channel review, PoE fault isolation, configuration recovery, and how to tell a bridge problem from a camera, switch, or local AP fault.

After three months, the workshop link was carrying its original devices plus four new sensors without becoming the site’s new complaint. The customer avoided trenching, but the real success was more ordinary: each link was sized for the work at its far end.

A CPE bridge is not chosen by asking how far it can claim to reach. It is chosen by deciding what cannot be allowed to fail.
