I am usually called after the polite explanations have failed.

Someone says the WiFi is “a little off.” Someone else says the signal bars look fine. A third person says maybe the internet provider had a bad day. By the time it reaches me, people are tired, the printer has become the enemy, and the room has started to behave like a place that resents being used.

That was the condition of our temporary NPI room in Guadalajara.

The room had been assembled the way many project spaces are assembled in factories: quickly, with practical furniture, and with the optimistic belief that if a room is small, it cannot possibly produce a difficult network problem. Small rooms live on that reputation. They do not deserve it.

This one carried three engineering laptops most days, a shared printer, several mobile phones, and the occasional customer notebook when review sessions were underway. Add cloud files, revised work instructions, photo uploads, messaging apps, and short video meetings, and the place developed the temper of a crowded bus stop.

The weird part was that nothing ever broke loudly. The network just lost its manners. Files stalled before opening. Calls turned thin and uneven. A browser page looked back at you as though it had not yet decided whether you deserved an answer. Print jobs lingered. Every interruption was minor. Together they could waste an hour and ruin the tone of a meeting.

The first bad guess was coverage. It often is. People trust distance because distance can be seen. But the room was not suffering from range. It was suffering from congestion in miniature. Too many small requests were arriving too close together, and the old setup handled them like a clerk faced with ten urgent questions before finishing the first one.

Once we called the problem by its proper name, the rest became clearer. We did not need to solve warehouse networking. We did not need roaming across departments. We did not need some oversized answer that made a small room sound grander than it was. We needed steadier behavior in a temporary project space where several terminals could wake up and demand attention at once.

We went with the COMFAST WR632AX for that reason.

I liked the decision because it respected the room. That may sound sentimental, but engineering goes wrong when you ignore the character of the place you are trying to fix. The NPI room was compact, busy, and timing-sensitive. It was not a general office. It was not casual space. It was a room where one delayed file could slow a discussion, where one dropped call could make a customer feel the whole project was loosely tied together.

Setup was uncomplicated. We placed the unit where the main work positions and the printer could connect cleanly, kept our expectations realistic, and did not ask a small-space router to pretend it was a site-wide infrastructure system. That alone prevented the usual disappointment that follows when a device is assigned the wrong job.

The result was not spectacular, which is another way of saying it was good. The room became quieter. Not quieter in sound, but in behavior. Files opened as though they understood the hour. Calls stopped wobbling without warning. People printed what they needed and returned to the conversation instead of waiting in annoyed silence. The network ceased to be a character in the room.

Temporary rooms appear everywhere in manufacturing. Pilot rooms. Review corners. Audit spaces. NPI tables disguised as meeting rooms. They all seem too modest to require careful networking decisions until the day they are full of devices and expectations. Then everyone discovers that a small room can become crowded very quickly.
