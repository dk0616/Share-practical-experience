I managed production at an automotive-components factory near Wrocław, Poland. For years, our network had been designed around a reasonable assumption: machines stayed where engineers installed them.

Then the factory became less reasonable.

We added automated guided vehicles, handheld quality terminals, smart cameras, environmental sensors, and a temporary inspection cell that moved whenever a new customer programme entered pilot production. Maintenance teams also began using tablets to access machine records on the floor.

Our Ethernet network remained important. PLCs, fixed workstations, production servers, and critical equipment continued using wired connections. The problem was not that cables had become obsolete.

The problem was that every production change had become a cabling project.

When the engineering department moved a temporary inspection station approximately 80 metres, facilities had to arrange cable routes, protective conduit, shutdown windows, and contractor access. A new AGV staging zone created the same discussion again.

Production wanted flexibility. IT wanted control. Maintenance wanted fewer temporary cables crossing working areas.

We began evaluating industrial 5G connectivity.

The first mistake would have been to ask only which router had the highest theoretical speed. Our AGVs were not downloading films. The more important questions concerned connection stability, response time, remote monitoring, device movement, backup communication, and how quickly a new work zone could be brought online.

That is the practical reasoning behind [industrial 5G router planning](https://wifioem.com/why-smart-factories-need-industrial-5g-routers/) . Smart factories need to identify which systems require mobility and adaptable connectivity instead of replacing every cable simply because wireless technology is available.

Our Polish system integrator worked with COMFAST engineers to review the plant.

We divided the factory into fixed production areas, mobile material-handling routes, temporary manufacturing cells, warehouse zones, engineering rooms, and an outdoor loading area. Wired networking remained the primary choice for many permanent machines.

5G routers were introduced where changing layouts created repeated installation work.

The pilot covered three applications.

The first was a temporary quality-inspection cell containing two industrial PCs, four smart cameras, and an edge gateway. The second supported maintenance tablets around a machining area. The third provided a secondary communication path for a warehouse monitoring system.

COMFAST engineers assisted remotely with router configuration, local network settings, device grouping, and testing procedures. We also kept production, engineering, maintenance, and monitoring traffic separated rather than placing every connected device into one flat network.

The pilot ran for six weeks.

During that period, engineering moved the inspection cell twice.

Previously, each move would have involved requesting a new data connection. This time, maintenance relocated the cell, reconnected local equipment, checked the 5G router, and resumed testing without installing another long cable route.

The value became clearer when one wired warehouse connection was damaged during maintenance work. The secondary wireless path kept environmental and status data available while the cable was repaired.

Nobody claimed that 5G should now replace the factory Ethernet network. In fact, the project convinced us of the opposite.

Fixed critical systems deserved stable wired infrastructure.

Mobile, temporary, difficult-to-cable, and backup applications deserved another option.

After the pilot, the factory purchased additional router units for temporary production cells, the warehouse edge, and two maintenance zones. COMFAST also provided remote technical training for our IT administrator and system integrator, covering configuration backup, SIM and network checks, local device troubleshooting, and the difference between an upstream mobile-network issue and a factory LAN problem.

Six months later, a new customer programme required a temporary assembly and inspection area.

The production manager received the request on Monday.

By Wednesday, the network was operating.

That would not have happened with our old assumption that every machine position should be designed as though it would remain there for ten years.

Smart manufacturing did not make cabling unnecessary.

It made flexibility part of the production requirement.
