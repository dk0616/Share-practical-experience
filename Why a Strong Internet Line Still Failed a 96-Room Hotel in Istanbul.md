The internet line was not the problem.

That was the first conclusion I reached after arriving at a 96-room business hotel on the European side of Istanbul. The property had a 1 Gbps fiber connection, yet guests regularly complained that video calls froze, television streams paused, and phones lost connection between the lift and their rooms.

The hotel manager had already spoken to the internet provider. The provider tested the line and found nothing unusual. At reception, a speed test showed more than 600 Mbps.

Everyone had evidence that the network was fast. The guests had evidence that it was not usable.

I was responsible for finding the difference.

The Building Had Been Treated as One Large Room

The hotel had seven guest floors, a restaurant, two meeting rooms, and a narrow lobby. Its original wireless network consisted of several consumer routers and range extenders placed near electrical outlets.

This arrangement appeared economical. It also created four separate problems.

First, the concrete walls between rooms weakened the signal. Second, the extenders repeated an already weak wireless connection. Third, each router worked independently, so guests’ phones often remained connected to a distant device. Fourth, the breakfast area became crowded between 7:30 and 9:30 every morning.

The network had coverage in the technical sense. A phone could detect a signal almost everywhere. However, signal detection and usable service are not the same thing.

Anyone examining similar [hotel WiFi deployment problems](https://wifioem.com/hotel-wifi-problems-enterprise-access-points/)  should begin by observing where guests actually use the network, rather than standing beside the main router with a test application.

We Tested the Guest’s Journey

COMFAST engineers asked us to divide the hotel into operating zones.

We tested beside the beds, behind closed room doors, at the ends of corridors, inside the breakfast room, in the lifts’ waiting areas, and at several tables in the conference rooms. We also walked from the lobby to the upper floors while maintaining a video call.

The results were more useful than the original speed tests.

Several rooms had acceptable download speed near the entrance but unstable service beside the bed. One corridor access device was serving too many rooms through two reinforced walls. The meeting rooms had good signal strength but insufficient capacity when more than 40 devices connected.

The design was revised around those findings.

The New Network Design

The hotel selected COMFAST indoor WiFi 6 access points for the guest floors and higher-capacity ceiling access points for the lobby, restaurant, and meeting areas. PoE switches supplied both power and data, removing the need for local adapters above the ceilings.

The access points were managed through a central gateway. Separate networks were created for guests, hotel staff, and operational equipment. Bandwidth limits prevented a small number of devices from occupying an unfair share of the connection.

The engineering team also adjusted radio power and channel settings. This mattered because installing more access points without coordination can create interference rather than solve it.

COMFAST provided remote configuration guidance before installation and held an online training session with the hotel’s maintenance contractor. The training covered AP adoption, channel inspection, client monitoring, firmware management, and basic fault diagnosis.

The hotel initially purchased enough equipment for two floors and the lobby. After a three-week trial, it approved the remaining deployment.

What Changed After Installation

During the first month, the front desk recorded fewer WiFi-related calls. The most noticeable change occurred in rooms at the ends of the corridors, where guests had previously reported frequent disconnections.

The meeting rooms also became easier to manage. During a 55-person corporate session, participants used cloud documents, messaging applications, and video services without the repeated reconnections seen under the previous system.

The hotel did not achieve this result by buying a faster internet package. It achieved it by treating rooms, corridors, restaurants, and meeting areas as different wireless environments.

That distinction is simple, but it is often ignored.

A hotel network is not successful because one test produces a large number. It is successful when guests can close the room door, open a laptop, walk to the lift, attend a meeting, and remain connected without thinking about the equipment above the ceiling.
